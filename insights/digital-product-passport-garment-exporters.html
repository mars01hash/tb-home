What the EU Digital Product Passport means for garment exporters

A plain-language guide to the DPP: what it is, when it is expected for textiles, and how to prepare your data.

TechBeez RMG solutions team 12 June 2025 8 min read

On this page

  1. What is a Digital Product Passport?
  2. Where it comes from
  3. What a textile passport is likely to include
  4. Who is responsible
  5. How garment exporters can prepare
  6. Key takeaways

The Digital Product Passport, or DPP, is one of the biggest changes coming to clothing sold in the European Union. For exporters, it turns sustainability and traceability from a document request into a data requirement.

NoteThis article summarises the position as of June 2025. The detailed rules for textiles are still being prepared by the European Commission, so check the latest official guidance before making decisions.

What is a Digital Product Passport?

A DPP is a structured digital record of information about a product, linked to the product itself through a data carrier such as a QR code. Customers, brands, recyclers and authorities can scan it to see information like material composition, origin, durability, repair and end-of-life handling.

Where it comes from

The DPP is set up by the EU's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR), which entered into force in July 2024. ESPR is a framework: the specific requirements for each product group are set later in separate acts. In April 2025, the Commission published its first working plan, which includes textiles and apparel among the priority product groups, with the textile rules expected around 2027 and a transition period after that.

What a textile passport is likely to include

The final list will be set in the textile rules, but based on ESPR and industry pilots, exporters should expect to provide data such as:

  • Fibre composition, including recycled content.
  • Where key steps took place: spinning, fabric production, dyeing and finishing, and garment making.
  • Information about substances of concern.
  • Care, repair and durability information.
  • Unique product and supplier identifiers.

Who is responsible

Legally, the obligation sits with the company placing the product on the EU market, usually the brand or importer. In practice, most of the data comes from the supply chain, so brands will pass requests on to their garment suppliers, and those suppliers will need the data from mills and trim suppliers.

How garment exporters can prepare

  1. Make data order-based. Every fabric, trim and process should be traceable to the order, style and colour it was used for.
  2. Collect supplier data in a structured way. Mill names and locations, certificate numbers and test results, captured as fields rather than buried in PDFs.
  3. Use consistent identifiers. Stable codes for styles, materials and suppliers make it possible to exchange data with buyers' systems.
  4. Talk to your buyers early. Many brands are already running DPP pilots and will specify formats. Agreeing them early avoids rework.

Exporters who already run their merchandising, sourcing and production on connected data will find the DPP a reporting task. Those who rely on scattered spreadsheets will find it a project.

Key takeaways

  • A DPP is a scannable digital record of a product's materials, origin and care.
  • It comes from the EU ESPR, in force since July 2024; textile rules are expected around 2027.
  • Brands are responsible, but the data comes from suppliers and their mills.
  • Start now by making material and supplier data order-based and structured.
