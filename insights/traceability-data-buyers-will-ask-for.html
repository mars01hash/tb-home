Traceability in RMG: the data your buyers will ask for next

Fibre origin, mills, processing steps and certificates. Why exporters should start structuring this data now.

TechBeez RMG solutions team 18 September 2024 6 min read

On this page

  1. Why buyers are asking now
  2. The data you are likely to be asked for
  3. Why this is hard today
  4. How to start structuring it
  5. Key takeaways

For years, a garment exporter's compliance file was mostly about the factory: social audits, safety certificates and test reports. That is changing. Buyers increasingly want to know about the product's whole journey, from fibre to finished garment.

Why buyers are asking now

Several pressures point in the same direction. Import rules in some markets already require evidence of where cotton comes from. In the EU, the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation entered into force in July 2024 and sets the framework for Digital Product Passports, with textiles expected to be one of the early product groups. Brands also face growing scrutiny of their environmental and social claims. All of this lands on suppliers as requests for data.

The data you are likely to be asked for

AreaTypical questions
FibreFibre composition, origin of cotton or other fibres, recycled or organic content.
Supply chainWhich spinner, mill, dyeing house and trim supplier made each component.
ProcessingWhere fabric was knitted or woven, dyed, printed and finished, and by whom.
CertificatesScope and transaction certificates for organic or recycled claims, test reports.
ProductionThe factory and lines that made the order, and any subcontracting.

Why this is hard today

Most of this information already exists, but it is scattered: in supplier invoices, certificate PDFs, booking sheets and emails. It is usually organised by supplier or by month, not by order. When a buyer asks which mill made the fabric for a specific style and lot, someone has to rebuild the answer by hand.

How to start structuring it

  1. Link every material purchase to an order and style. If fabric and trims are booked against orders, traceability follows naturally.
  2. Record suppliers at component level. Not just "fabric supplier" but the mill, and where possible the spinner.
  3. Store certificates as data, not only as files. Capture certificate type, number, scope and expiry date so they can be matched to purchases.
  4. Ask suppliers for the same fields every time. A short, consistent data request is easier for suppliers than ad-hoc questions per order.
Think order firstIf you can open one order and see every material, supplier and certificate behind it, you can answer almost any traceability question your buyers will ask.

Key takeaways

  • Buyers are moving from factory audits to product-level traceability.
  • The EU's ESPR framework, in force since July 2024, makes this more pressing for textiles.
  • Link purchases, suppliers and certificates to each order and style now.
